## final.course_reflection
What stood out most was how often the robot looked correct without actually being right. In Mission 1 my color rule drew a confident box on the dim image, and the presence check counted it as a hit, even though the box covered the whole desk instead of the mug. That made me more careful about what a metric is really measuring. The robot missions were the most engaging part, because one change, raising the line-following gain from 0.08 to 0.8, turned a robot that drifted off the bend into one that reached the finish. Seeing perception and control fail in different ways, and being able to tell which from the logs, made debugging feel like engineering rather than guessing.

The activity also made me think about the people who would share space with robots like these. A delivery or assistive robot that only sees floor paint or a colored blob cannot tell whether someone is standing in its path. Connecting technical choices to safety and trust feels essential, not optional, and it makes me more motivated to keep learning about perception and testing for robots that work around people.

## final.synthesis
Across the three missions, the main lesson is that a camera only gives evidence that some pixels match a rule or a model. A reliable behavior depends on how that evidence was produced, whether it still holds when conditions change, and how the controller acts on it.

In Mission 1, my locked classical rule (H 0-35, S 70-255, V 25-110) boxed the mug correctly on normal_1 only because the mug was darker than the bright wood. On dim_1 the whole desk fell under the same brightness ceiling, merged with the mug into one 53,722 px^2 contour, and the largest-area rule boxed the desk. The presence check still called it a TP, but box matching failed (dim: 0 correct, 2 false, 2 missed). Overall, it had 16 correct, 4 false and 4 missed boxes. The learned YOLOX detector depended heavily on its acceptance threshold. At 0.50 it found only 1 of the 6 mugs in normal/dim/occluded. At 0.05 it found all 6 with no false boxes (16/2/4 over the full set) but took about 32-36 ms per image versus about 3 ms. The class-agnostic foreground mask on normal_1 reached only IoU 0.576 / Dice 0.731 and had no label, so a detailed boundary still did not say which object was the mug.

In Mission 2, with the same kind of HSV rule closed in a loop, the robot centered and held all four pillars (attempt 20261031T001016-ad4efd3e, gain 1.2, complete in 21.5 s), and the turn direction always matched the sign of the image error (blue on the left gave positive, left, turn). Doubling only the gain to 2.4 (20261031T001537-c9fea784) did not speed up the task (21.4 s), because SEARCH dominates. It made the turn rate go negative and broke a CONFIRM on turquoise, so more gain was not better.

In Mission 3, with GAIN 0.08 the mask kept finding the magenta line (TRACK every step), but the turn command stayed under 0.06 rad/s, the error drifted toward +1 on the bend, and the robot never reached the finish (20261031T002618-6e7c854c). Changing only GAIN to 0.8 completed the curve (20261031T003001-274ed4d4, 126.4 s, mean |error| 0.072 vs 0.49, no line loss).

Perception and control fail in different ways, and the logs let me tell which. In Mission 1 the perception rule itself broke under a lighting change. In Mission 3 perception was fine and the controller was under-tuned. In Mission 2 an over-tuned controller added overshoot. A single threshold or gain that works on one condition (normal lighting, a straight strip) does not transfer automatically, so each choice has to be tested on the condition it is meant to handle. The safety behaviors (WAIT_CAMERA, LINE_LOST, the velocity guard) were what kept failures harmless.

Every method here works in image coordinates only, with no depth, no calibrated distance and no object identity beyond a color or a broad class. All results come from a small set of images and a single simulated world with one start pose per run. A real assistive or delivery robot would need depth or range sensing, independent safety stops, and far larger and more varied tests before trusting any of these choices.

## line_following.comparison
All three attempts used the same settings except the gain: SPEED 0.16, ROI 0.60-0.95, hue 140-169, MIN_FRACTION 0.002, steering = -gain x error.

Straight segment, practice 20261031T002241-7308b2bf (GAIN 0.08): stable tracking. The magenta strip stayed inside the processing region the whole time (TRACK for 687 steps), mean |error| was only 0.049 and the turn commands were tiny (about -0.02 to +0.03 rad/s). On a straight line the error is already near zero, so even a weak gain is enough. It only left TRACK at the end of the strip (32 LINE_LOST steps, 1 line-loss event) and I stopped it with Ctrl+C after 71.8 s.

Curved track, same program, 20261031T002618-6e7c854c (GAIN 0.08): too weak on the bends. It stayed in TRACK for all 857 steps, so the mask still found the magenta line, but mean |error| rose to 0.49 and the error plot climbs toward +1.0, meaning the line drifted out to the right edge of the processing region. The turn commands never exceeded about +/-0.06 rad/s, so the robot kept going almost straight while the line curved away. It never reached the red finish (stopped by Ctrl+C after 85.6 s, outcome interrupted).

Change: I edited only GAIN from 0.08 to 0.8, saved, reset the track world and reran from the same start pose: 20261031T003001-274ed4d4. This attempt completed: TRACK 1265 steps then COMPLETE at the red finish panel after 126.4 s, 0 line-loss events, and mean |error| dropped to 0.072 (about 7x smaller). The evidence that the gain caused this: the turn-rate plot now reaches about +0.4 rad/s on the bend instead of 0.06, and the error stays between about -0.6 and +0.2 instead of running out to +1. A ten-times larger gain gives ten times more turn for the same image error, enough to follow the curvature. I did not see sustained left-right oscillation at 0.8. The turn rate dipped only a little below zero (to about -0.1), so it corrected without large overshoot. It took longer in simulation time than the failed run because the controller slows forward speed when the error is large and it actually drove the whole curve.

## line_following.limits
Failure: the curved-track attempt 20261031T002618-6e7c854c with GAIN 0.08 never reached the finish and I had to stop it after 85.6 s. This began in the control, not in image processing. The perception side was working: every decision was TRACK (no LINE_LOST, no CODE_ERROR), so a valid magenta contour was found in every fresh frame and the error had the correct sign (line to the right = positive error). The problem was that the proportional command -0.08 x error was too small to turn the robot as fast as the line curved, so the error kept growing toward +1. Raising only the gain to 0.8 fixed it with the same mask settings, which confirms the cause was the controller.

A second, intended stop: the practice run entered LINE_LOST at the end of the straight strip. That is missing information. The line simply ended and there was no finish marker, so the robot correctly stopped instead of guessing. The 1 to 9 WAIT_CAMERA steps at the start of each run also show it refusing to move until fresh images arrive.

For a real delivery robot around people this is not enough. The controller only sees a floor colour in one camera. It has no idea whether a person, pet or bag is standing on the line, it does not know its distance to anything, and the red stop panel is just a colour cue. It would need obstacle and person detection with range sensing (lidar or a depth camera, plus bumpers or a safety-rated scanner), localization on a map so it is not totally dependent on paint, a speed limit and a certified emergency stop that do not depend on the vision software, and more robust line detection (lighting changes, worn or dirty paint, shadows, similar-coloured objects). Tests would include many lighting conditions and floor surfaces, partly covered or missing line sections, people stepping in front at different distances and speeds, camera dropout and latency, sensor failure, and supervised trials in the real building before any unsupervised use.

## mission_1.diagnosis
normal_1: Yes, the box is on the mug. With my locked rule (H 0-35, S 70-255, V 25-110, kernel 3, min area 300) the bright wood is mostly above V=110, so it drops out of the mask. The darker brown mug body and its shadow stay white, and after morphology they form contour 5 (area about 13,549 px^2, circularity 0.30, centroid near (314, 68)). That is the largest eligible region, so it wins and the green box lands on the mug (box matching passed for the normal condition).

dim_1: No, the box is not on the mug. Darkening the whole image pushes the wood desk below V=110 too, so the desk now passes the same rule as the mug. In the mask almost the entire desk is white and it merges with the mug into one huge region, contour 11 (area about 53,722 px^2, circularity only 0.05, centroid near (307, 167) in the middle of the desk). Because the detector picks the largest eligible area, this desk blob wins and the box covers nearly the whole image. The relevant setting is the value upper bound (110): it separated mug from wood only at normal brightness. The presence check still counted this as a TP, but box matching failed for both dim images (box_tp 0, box_fp 2).

## mission_1.limitations
I changed only the value (brightness) upper bound, from 180 down to 110. With V up to 180 the orange-brown wood passed along with the mug, so the selected region (contour 11, about 17,577 px^2) was the mug merged with a big patch of desk and the box stretched across the wood to the right edge. Lowering the upper bound removed the brighter wood pixels from the mask, the mug separated from the desk, and the selected contour became the mug and its shadow (contour 5, about 13,549 px^2), so the box tightened onto the mug.

Limitation: the rule only knows color and brightness, not "mug". It worked because this mug happens to be darker than this desk under this lighting. A white, blue or bright-colored cup would fall outside the hue/value range entirely, and a dark background or dim lighting (like dim_1) makes the background pass the same thresholds so the largest-area rule picks the background. It also still includes the mug's shadow, and it would pick any other dark brown object of similar size. A fixed threshold does not transfer to a different cup, background or lighting without retuning.

## mission_2.choice
I recommend the learned YOLOX detector with a low acceptance threshold of 0.05. On normal, dim, and occluded images, it found all 6 mugs with 0 false boxes, while the classical rule failed on both dim images. Across all images at 0.05, the learned detector had 16 correct, 2 false, and 4 missed boxes. It had the same box recall with fewer wrong-object boxes. The cost is time: about 32-36 ms per image versus about 3 ms for the classical rule. That is still fast for a desk-assistance robot that does not need to react in milliseconds. A threshold this low should be watched for false boxes in more cluttered scenes.

On normal_1, the class-agnostic U2-Net foreground mask at threshold 0.50 has mask IoU 0.576 and Dice 0.731 against the visible-mug mask, with 4,453 extra pixels and 1,852 missed mug pixels. The mask has no class label: it marks whatever is visually noticeable, so the extra pixels come from other desk items and background, and a high IoU just means the regions overlap. It cannot tell the robot which foreground blob is the mug versus the laptop or keyboard, so it would need a detector or classifier to supply the identity, plus depth and calibration before any grasp.

Important limit: this is a very small test (31 images, only 2 mug images per condition, one mug and one desk scene with mostly synthetic changes like dimming and occlusion). A single image changes a condition's score by 50%, and the result may not generalize to other mugs, desks, real lighting, or real camera viewpoints.

## pillar_search.evidence
Four-color attempt 20261031T001016-ad4efd3e (red, green, turquoise, blue; gain 1.2): all four colors were centered and held, and the outcome was complete in 21.5 s of simulation time, with visited_colors = red, green, turquoise, blue and forward speed 0 throughout. None were missed.

State timeline: WAIT_CAMERA x3 at the start (the controller refused to move until fresh frames arrived), then for each color the same pattern: red SEARCH 32 -> CENTER 12 -> CONFIRM 3 -> HOLD 8 -> NEXT; green SEARCH 29 -> CENTER 13 -> CONFIRM 3 -> HOLD 7 -> NEXT; turquoise SEARCH 30 -> CENTER 14 -> CONFIRM 2 -> HOLD 7 -> NEXT; blue SEARCH 30 -> CENTER 13 -> CONFIRM 2 -> HOLD 7 -> COMPLETE. The similar SEARCH lengths fit four pillars spaced roughly evenly around the robot, each needing about a quarter turn at the search speed. In the camera evidence the mask only shows the requested color inside the processing region, the yellow centroid moves toward the magenta center during CENTER, and the error plot drops to near 0 before CONFIRM and HOLD. Mean |error| while a target was visible was 0.347 (normalized image units, not metres).

Why this is not proof of identity or distance: the controller only knows that a large enough connected region of pixels fell inside an HSV range and that its centroid was near the image center for a few frames. Any object of the same color (a turquoise wall, a poster, another robot) would produce the same mask and the same HOLD. A colored region is not the same as a pillar. The error is a horizontal image coordinate, so centering only tells us bearing (the pillar is straight ahead), not how far away it is: a small near object and a big far one can look the same. A stable hold just means the image did not change while the robot stood still. Distance would need depth, a known object size with camera calibration, or another sensor.
